# Editorial Motion

## Purpose

Use this skill when a website should feel designed, edited and composed like a living publication rather than assembled from standard web components.

The objective is to combine:

- editorial design
- typography
- photography
- print artifacts
- spatial composition
- motion
- scrolling
- storytelling

The page should feel authored.

Not templated.

---

# 1. CORE PRINCIPLE

Do not think only in:

sections
containers
cards
components

Think in:

pages
spreads
covers
columns
captions
clippings
photographs
headlines
margins
layers
sequences

A website may behave like a publication without literally imitating paper.

---

# 2. EDITORIAL HIERARCHY

Every composition needs hierarchy.

Possible levels:

DISPLAY
Major visual statement.

HEADLINE
Primary editorial title.

DECK
Supporting introduction.

BODY
Long-form reading.

CAPTION
Image information.

METADATA
Date, category, author, archive number.

ANNOTATION
Small contextual notes.

Hierarchy should remain recognizable even when the composition is experimental.

---

# 3. TYPOGRAPHY AS IMAGE

Typography may function as a visual object.

Use:

- extreme scale
- cropping
- overlap
- rotation
- vertical orientation
- repetition
- tracking
- contrast
- negative space

Large words may extend beyond the viewport.

Text may partially disappear behind photography.

But important information must remain understandable.

---

# 4. SCALE CONTRAST

Editorial design benefits from dramatic scale contrast.

Example:

very large headline

paired with

very small metadata.

Avoid making every text element medium-sized.

Create rhythm through contrast.

---

# 5. ALIGNMENT

Not everything must be centered.

Use:

- left alignment
- right alignment
- baseline relationships
- edge alignment
- intentional misalignment
- vertical text

Asymmetry should feel composed, not accidental.

---

# 6. GRID

Start from a grid.

Then break it intentionally.

Possible systems:

- 6-column
- 8-column
- 12-column
- modular grid
- asymmetric grid

Objects may cross columns.

Photography may escape the grid.

Headlines may span multiple regions.

Breaking a grid only feels powerful when a grid exists underneath.

---

# 7. WHITESPACE

Whitespace is active composition.

Use empty areas to:

- create tension
- isolate photography
- increase importance
- establish rhythm
- slow the reader

Do not fill every empty area.

---

# 8. PHOTOGRAPHY

Photography is not decoration.

It can establish:

- mood
- time
- place
- identity
- cultural context

Allow photography to dominate when appropriate.

Possible treatments:

- fullscreen
- cropped
- oversized
- partially hidden
- overlapping
- contact sheet
- print
- scan
- film frame

---

# 9. IMAGE CROPPING

Cropping can create tension.

Possible approaches:

- cut faces partially
- crop architecture
- show fragments
- use extreme close-ups
- reveal only part of an object

Never crop important documentary information accidentally.

---

# 10. IMAGE LAYERS

Images may overlap:

- other images
- typography
- paper
- navigation
- metadata

Use depth intentionally.

Important text must retain sufficient readability.

---

# 11. PRINT ARTIFACTS

Use physical-media references where they strengthen the editorial concept.

Examples:

- magazine pages
- newspaper clippings
- flyers
- posters
- tickets
- receipts
- labels
- photocopies
- contact sheets
- album inserts
- handwritten notes

These should behave like content objects rather than decorative stickers.

---

# 12. NEWSPAPER CLIPPINGS

Clippings may:

- introduce historical context
- connect related stories
- show headlines
- act as navigation
- reveal source material

Possible behavior:

initially rotated

hover
→ straighten slightly

click
→ enlarge / inspect

drag
→ reveal material underneath

Maintain source attribution when using real archival material.

---

# 13. MAGAZINE LANGUAGE

Magazine-inspired compositions may use:

- page numbers
- issue numbers
- section labels
- running headers
- captions
- pull quotes
- folios
- editorial indexes
- credits

These details create publication identity.

Do not add fake metadata merely for decoration.

---

# 14. COVER MOMENTS

Important sections may behave like magazine covers.

A cover may contain:

- dominant photograph
- publication title
- issue/date
- primary headline
- secondary headlines

Use cover moments sparingly.

They should signal important transitions.

---

# 15. CONTACT SHEETS

Contact sheets are useful for:

- photography archives
- fashion collections
- event documentation
- visual research

They may include:

frame number
date
location
photographer

Allow inspection of individual frames.

---

# 16. SCANNED MATERIAL

Scanned artifacts may include:

- slight rotation
- uneven edges
- paper texture
- dust
- subtle imperfections
- faded printing

Avoid exaggerated fake damage.

The goal is material authenticity, not a "vintage filter."

---

# 17. TEXTURE

Texture may communicate medium.

Examples:

paper grain

photocopy noise

film grain

print dots

scanner artifacts

Use subtly.

Texture should not reduce readability.

---

# 18. LONG-FORM READING

Experimental art direction must not destroy article readability.

For long paragraphs:

- maintain comfortable measure
- adequate line height
- sufficient contrast
- predictable reading flow

Use experimental typography around the article.

Do not make the entire body copy experimental.

---

# 19. ARTICLE OPENING

Article intros may be highly expressive.

Possible sequence:

category
→ date
→ headline
→ deck
→ photography
→ body

Or:

photograph
→ headline appears
→ metadata reveals
→ article begins

The transition into body reading should be clear.

---

# 20. CHAPTERS

Long stories may be divided into editorial chapters.

Example:

01
THE CITY

02
THE SOUND

03
THE CLOTHES

04
THE IMAGE

Chapter transitions can change:

- layout
- typography
- image treatment
- motion rhythm

while remaining within the same design system.

---

# 21. PULL QUOTES

Use pull quotes to create rhythm in long-form content.

They may:

- interrupt columns
- overlap imagery
- become oversized
- appear in margins

Never invent quotations.

Clearly attribute real quotations.

---

# 22. CAPTIONS

Photography should have meaningful captions when context matters.

Possible information:

photographer

year

location

subject

publication

source

Keep captions visually secondary but accessible.

---

# 23. METADATA

Metadata is a strong editorial design material.

Examples:

ARCHIVE_012

MUSIC / 1998

LONDON

ISSUE 03

FRAME 07

Use metadata consistently.

Do not create meaningless technical-looking numbers.

---

# 24. MOTION PHILOSOPHY

Motion should behave like editorial sequencing.

Think:

reveal
turn
slide
unfold
focus
layer
crop
assemble

Avoid treating every object like a generic UI component entering with:

fade-up
fade-up
fade-up
fade-up

Motion needs rhythm.

---

# 25. SCROLL RHYTHM

Scroll should alternate between:

dense information

and

visual breathing room.

Possible rhythm:

headline
→ image
→ text
→ whitespace
→ clipping
→ image sequence
→ text

Do not maintain identical section heights throughout the experience.

---

# 26. STICKY COMPOSITION

Sticky positioning may create editorial sequences.

Examples:

headline remains while images change

photograph remains while captions progress

year remains while archive items scroll

Use sticky elements only when they clarify relationships.

Do not trap content unnecessarily.

---

# 27. IMAGE REVEALS

Avoid always fading images in.

Possible reveals:

- mask expansion
- crop movement
- paper sliding away
- focus change
- image emerging from another layer
- contact sheet selection

Choose reveals appropriate to the medium being represented.

---

# 28. TEXT REVEALS

Text may appear through:

- line reveal
- mask
- clipping
- tracking change
- opacity
- controlled vertical movement

Avoid excessive character-by-character animation in long text.

Reserve detailed typography animation for major headlines.

---

# 29. PAPER MOTION

Printed objects may:

- slide
- rotate
- stack
- unfold
- lift
- shift
- reveal another object

Movement should imply weight and friction.

A newspaper should not move like a glass panel.

---

# 30. PHOTOGRAPH MOTION

Photographs may feel lighter or heavier depending on their presentation.

Possible behaviors:

print
→ draggable

film strip
→ horizontal movement

contact sheet
→ zoom inspection

fullscreen image
→ slow cinematic movement

Match motion to the physical metaphor.

---

# 31. TRANSITIONS

Page transitions may originate from editorial objects.

Examples:

album cover
→ becomes article hero

magazine
→ opens into story

photograph
→ expands into gallery

headline
→ remains while page changes

Transitions should preserve visual continuity.

---

# 32. ARCHIVE DESIGN

Archives should not automatically become card grids.

Consider:

- chronological indexes
- issue tables
- vertical timelines
- image walls
- contact sheets
- typographic lists
- shelves
- stacks
- filing systems

Choose the structure based on the content.

---

# 33. ARCHIVE FILTERS

Filtering can be expressed editorially.

Examples:

ALL
MUSIC
FASHION
PHOTOGRAPHY
CULTURE

Filters should remain understandable.

Experimental presentation must not obscure basic functionality.

---

# 34. HORIZONTAL COMPOSITIONS

Horizontal sequences may be appropriate for:

- timelines
- magazine spreads
- photo essays
- fashion lookbooks

Do not replace normal vertical scrolling across an entire site without strong reason.

Horizontal sections should have clear interaction cues.

---

# 35. COLLAGE

Collage can combine:

- photography
- typography
- clippings
- labels
- scanned objects

Maintain hierarchy.

Randomly scattering elements is not art direction.

Every position should feel intentional.

---

# 36. CONTROLLED IMPERFECTION

Editorial layouts may include slight irregularity.

Examples:

2-degree rotation

uneven crop

offset baseline

imperfect paper edge

Do not randomize everything.

Imperfection should feel authored.

---

# 37. RESPONSIVE EDITORIAL DESIGN

Do not merely shrink desktop compositions.

Desktop:

- complex spreads
- overlap
- wide typography
- spatial composition

Tablet:

- simplified spreads
- controlled overlap

Mobile:

- vertical editorial sequence
- large typography
- intentional cropping
- fewer simultaneous layers

Preserve editorial identity on every viewport.

---

# 38. MOBILE TYPOGRAPHY

Large type may remain large on mobile.

Do not automatically reduce every heading to conventional mobile sizes.

Instead:

- allow cropping
- use line breaks
- use vertical rhythm
- control overflow intentionally

Readability remains mandatory.

---

# 39. MOTION ON MOBILE

Simplify motion when necessary.

Prefer:

- masks
- opacity
- transforms
- controlled sticky sequences

Reduce:

- large parallax systems
- simultaneous moving layers
- heavy filters

The editorial experience should remain expressive.

---

# 40. REDUCED MOTION

Respect:

prefers-reduced-motion: reduce

Replace:

scroll-linked movement
→ static composition

complex reveal
→ simple appearance

paper movement
→ immediate state change

Never remove editorial content.

---

# 41. ACCESSIBILITY

Editorial experimentation must preserve:

- semantic headings
- reading order
- alt text
- keyboard navigation
- visible focus
- readable contrast

Visual order and DOM reading order should remain logically compatible.

Do not create an impressive collage that becomes nonsense to assistive technology.

---

# 42. SOURCE INTEGRITY

When displaying archival material:

- identify source
- identify publication when known
- identify date when known
- identify photographer when known
- distinguish reproduction from recreation

Never create fake historical evidence.

A newly designed graphic inspired by an era must not be presented as an authentic historical artifact.

---

# 43. COPYRIGHT AWARENESS

Do not assume that an image being available online means it is free to republish.

For production:

- verify usage rights
- prefer licensed assets
- prefer public-domain material when appropriate
- attribute when required
- preserve source records

During prototyping, clearly distinguish temporary reference assets from production-ready assets.

---

# 44. ANTI-GENERIC RULE

Avoid defaulting to:

CARD
CARD
CARD

SECTION
SECTION
SECTION

IMAGE LEFT
TEXT RIGHT

IMAGE RIGHT
TEXT LEFT

three-column grids

generic carousels

standard blog templates

Ask:

Could this composition exist in a printed cultural magazine?

Could it feel intentionally art-directed?

If not, reconsider the layout.

---

# 45. ANTI-CHAOS RULE

Experimental does not mean random.

Every unusual choice should support:

- hierarchy
- story
- rhythm
- medium
- atmosphere

If an element is rotated, ask why.

If elements overlap, ask why.

If typography is huge, ask why.

Intentionality separates editorial design from clutter.

---

# 46. PERFORMANCE

Editorial richness must remain efficient.

Optimize:

- images
- textures
- filters
- masks
- animations

Lazy-load large editorial photography.

Avoid dozens of simultaneous high-resolution images.

Use CSS effects instead of raster assets when appropriate.

---

# 47. EDITORIAL REVIEW

Before approving a page:

[ ] Is there a clear hierarchy?

[ ] Does typography contribute to the composition?

[ ] Is photography treated as content rather than decoration?

[ ] Does whitespace have purpose?

[ ] Are print artifacts meaningful?

[ ] Is motion varied rather than repetitive?

[ ] Does scrolling have rhythm?

[ ] Is body text comfortable to read?

[ ] Is the layout intentionally composed?

[ ] Does mobile retain editorial character?

[ ] Are historical materials properly distinguished?

[ ] Are sources and credits preserved?

[ ] Is accessibility maintained?

[ ] Is performance acceptable?

---

# FINAL PRINCIPLE

Do not assemble pages.

Art-direct them.

Typography is an image.

Photography is a voice.

Whitespace is pacing.

Motion is editing.

The browser is the publication.
